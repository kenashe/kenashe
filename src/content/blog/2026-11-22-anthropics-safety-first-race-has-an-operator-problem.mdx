TL;DR: Anthropic can be both sincere about AI safety and commercially aggressive, but builders should judge Claude on product behavior, reliability, and governance fit, not on the company’s moral branding.

## What is the actual contradiction?

Matthew Berman’s “I’m Crashing Out About Anthropic” is not a neutral company profile. It is a rant, clearly labeled by tone if not by format. But the critique hits a real tension: Anthropic was founded by OpenAI defectors who, by Berman’s telling, believed OpenAI was not taking AI safety seriously enough, then built one of the most important commercial AI labs in the world.

That is not hypocrisy by itself. Safety labs still need to ship. If you think frontier AI is dangerous, one reasonable strategy is to build it inside an organization that treats the danger seriously. The uncomfortable part is what happens next. The safer-builder argument becomes harder to parse when the same company warns about major labor disruption, powerful cyber capability, model welfare, and possible “life-like” systems, while also pushing product releases, enterprise adoption, and frontier competition.

Berman’s sharpest point is not “Anthropic bad.” He explicitly says he knows Anthropic people, has worked with Anthropic access, and believes many employees are genuine. His charge is narrower: the company’s story contains contradictions that matter because Anthropic asks the public, customers, regulators, and employees to treat its safety posture as part of the product.

That is the operator issue. Corporate intent is not an eval. Mission statements do not tell you if Claude will follow your internal policy, resist prompt injection, preserve context across a workflow, or avoid inventing citations in a customer-facing system.

## Does safety messaging excuse speed?

No. It also does not automatically discredit it.

A frontier lab can warn about risk and still build. In fact, most serious AI labs do exactly that now. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and others all publish safety language while competing on capability. The question is whether the warnings are matched by evidence, constraints, and behavior.

Berman points to an Anthropic pattern he sees as alarm, then release. He cites Dario Amodei’s reported warning about a “white collar blood bath,” while Anthropic keeps building tools that could automate parts of white-collar work. He also describes a “mythos” release cycle where, in his telling, Anthropic suggested a model was too dangerous to release, then later released it and kept iterating.

I would be careful with the second claim. The provided material does not include Anthropic’s own announcement, system card, or policy language for that release. So I would not treat Berman’s strongest framing as settled fact. But the broader dynamic is familiar: frontier labs often communicate in two registers at once. To policymakers, the model is dangerous enough to justify special treatment. To customers, it is safe enough to deploy. To investors, it is powerful enough to define a market. To researchers, it is controlled enough to study.

That can be true in pieces. It can also become narrative arbitrage.

For builders, the practical response is simple: ignore the aura. Read the system cards when available. Run your own red-team prompts. Test refusal behavior on your actual use case. Compare outputs against OpenAI, Gemini, Qwen, Mistral, and local models where appropriate. Anthropic’s safety branding may correlate with useful product behavior, especially in enterprise contexts, but it is not a substitute for workload-specific testing.

## What should builders do with Claude now?

Use it if it works. Do not join the religion.

Claude remains a serious product line, especially for writing, long-context work, coding assistance, analysis, and structured workflows. A company can have a complicated public posture and still ship useful models. The reverse is also true: a company can sound open, humble, or pro-builder and still produce brittle systems.

The right posture is vendor realism. Anthropic is not your ethics department. It is not your research monastery. It is not your enemy either. It is a frontier AI company with incentives: safety, revenue, talent, distribution, compute access, policy influence, and category leadership. Those incentives will not always point in the same direction.

So treat Claude like infrastructure with personality. Put it behind evals. Track regressions between model versions. Keep logs that your team can audit. Use model routing where the task allows it. Keep a second provider warm for critical workflows. If you are building agents, test tool-use failures and permission boundaries before you test the happy path.

The catch most readers miss: the Anthropic debate is not mainly about whether its leaders are sincere. They probably are. The useful question is whether your system still behaves well when the lab’s incentives, your user’s incentives, and the model’s learned behavior diverge. Try Claude on the work where its caution helps, measure it against the work where caution gets in the way, and make the switching cost low before you need it.
