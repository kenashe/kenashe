OpenAI announced Project Camellia this week, a datacenter build in Effingham County, Georgia. The blog post hits the notes you would expect: responsible energy, community investment, local jobs, and access to Codex for the people who live nearby. It reads like a good-neighbor pledge. And it is worth reading exactly that way, as a pledge, not a result.

I want to take the announcement seriously without taking it at face value. Because the real story here is not that OpenAI is building compute in rural Georgia. Everyone is building compute everywhere. The story is what a county gives up and gets back when a hyperscale build lands in its backyard, and whether the promises in a launch post survive contact with a power grid and a tax assessor.

## What the pledge actually says

Strip the announcement down and you get four commitments: energy done responsibly, money spent in the community, jobs created, and access to Codex for locals.

Three of those are measurable if anyone bothers to measure them. The fourth, "access to Codex," is the softest and the most interesting. A datacenter does not need local software engineers to run. So offering Codex access reads less like a business necessity and more like a signal: we want to be seen as bringing capability, not just concrete and cooling.

That distinction matters. Datacenters are famously stingy on permanent jobs. The construction phase employs hundreds or thousands. The operating phase often employs dozens. Effingham County residents should ask, and OpenAI should answer, what the steady-state headcount looks like after the cranes leave.

## The energy question is the whole question

"Responsible energy" is the phrase doing the most work and getting the least detail. In Georgia, that phrase runs straight into Georgia Power and the broader load growth that utilities across the Southeast are now scrambling to serve.

Here is the tension nobody in a launch post wants to spell out. A large datacenter can draw hundreds of megawatts. That demand does not appear from nowhere. It gets served by the existing grid, by new generation, or by the datacenter bringing its own power. Each path has a bill, and the question is who pays it.

If new gas plants or grid upgrades get built to serve the load and the cost lands in everyone's utility rates, then "community investment" on one line of the announcement can quietly become a rate increase on another. If OpenAI funds its own generation or pays the full marginal cost of grid expansion, that is a genuinely responsible build. The announcement does not say which. Watch the Georgia Public Service Commission filings, not the blog post, to find out.

I am not assuming the worst. Some operators are doing this well, signing power purchase agreements for new clean generation and paying for the interconnection they cause. But "responsible energy" is a claim, and claims in this category are cheap until a regulatory filing makes them expensive.

## Why rural counties keep saying yes

Effingham County is not being dragged into this. Rural counties compete hard for these projects, and it helps to understand why before judging whether the deal is good.

A hyperscale datacenter is a rare thing for a small tax base: a very large capital asset that does not fill up the schools, does not clog the roads at rush hour, and pays property tax for decades. For a county that has spent years watching young people leave for Atlanta or Savannah, a nine or ten figure investment with a marquee name attached is not nothing.

The catch is the incentive package. These deals almost always come with tax abatements, and the abatements are how a "billion dollar investment" can turn into a modest actual contribution to local revenue for the first ten or fifteen years. The public rarely sees those terms clearly at announcement time. If the abatement is generous and the permanent jobs are few and the power costs get socialized, the county has traded its land and grid capacity for a headline.

None of that is inevitable. Some of these deals are genuinely good for the community. The point is that the goodness lives entirely in the fine print, and the fine print is exactly what a "building with the community" post leaves out.

## The Codex angle is a tell worth reading

Back to that softest promise. OpenAI bundling Codex access into an infrastructure announcement is a small move that says something larger about how these companies want to be positioned.

Datacenters are increasingly a political liability. They draw power, they draw water, and they draw scrutiny. So the framing is shifting from "we are building industrial infrastructure" to "we are bringing the tools of the future to your town." Offering the local workforce access to the same coding tools the company sells is a way to make an abstract build feel personal and generative rather than extractive.

Whether that lands depends on follow-through. Access to Codex is meaningful if it comes with training, with actual programs in local schools and community colleges, with a path from "you can use this" to "people here got hired because of this." Access as a login and a link is a marketing line. Access as a pipeline is a real investment. We will know which one this is in about eighteen months.

## Practitioner's take

If you are a builder, the useful move here is not to cheer or jeer the announcement. It is to learn how to read one. Every hyperscale build now comes wrapped in the same four promises, and the promises are increasingly detached from the disclosures that would let you verify them.

So build the habit: when you see a datacenter announcement in your region, skip the blog post and go find three documents. The state utility commission filing that shows who pays for the new load. The county incentive agreement that shows the tax abatement terms and any jobs clawback if targets are missed. And the actual workforce commitment with numbers, not adjectives. If all three are strong, it is a good deal and worth supporting. If the announcement is loud and the filings are quiet, that gap is the story.

The catch most readers miss: the parts of these projects that create real local value, the permanent jobs, the funded power, the training pipeline, are the parts that cost the company money and never make the launch post. Effingham County's outcome is not decided by what OpenAI announced this week. It is decided by what shows up in the filings over the next two years. Watch those.
