TL;DR: The OCC’s first AI examiner guide signals that banks will be judged less on whether they “use AI” and more on how much autonomy those systems have, who controls them, and what damage they can cause.

## What did the OCC just put on the table?

Decrypt’s “US Bank Regulator Issues First AI Framework as Banks Battle Rogue Agents” reports on the OCC’s first examiner guide for AI. The important phrase is not “AI framework.” It is “examiner guide.”

That means this is not just guidance for innovation teams. It is a lens bank supervisors can use when they inspect how a bank manages AI risk. According to Decrypt, the OCC guide weighs autonomy and financial harm, and directly addresses outside AI agents.

That is the right axis.

For years, bank AI governance has mostly sounded like model governance: validation, bias testing, explainability, vendor review, documentation, controls. All real. All necessary. But agentic systems add a different question: can the system act?

A chatbot that gives a bad answer can create customer harm. An agent that takes steps through an account flow can create operational and financial harm much faster. The problem is not just whether the model is “accurate.” It is whether the surrounding system lets a probabilistic tool touch money, credentials, account settings, transfers, customer data, or support workflows without enough friction.

Decrypt also reports that the same risk recently pushed major banks to restrict customer-account access by outside AI agents. That detail matters because the agent wave is not only coming from inside banks. It is also arriving through consumer tools that sit outside the bank and ask users for permission, access, or credentials.

## Why are outside AI agents harder than bank-owned chatbots?

A bank-owned chatbot is annoying when it hallucinates. It is dangerous when it is wired into account actions without controls. But at least the bank owns the interface, the logs, the vendor relationship, the access layer, and the remediation path.

Outside agents are messier.

They may arrive through a customer, not a procurement process. They may not identify themselves cleanly. They may act through normal web or app flows. They may create traffic that looks like the customer, because from the bank’s point of view, it may be tied to the customer’s session or credentials.

That collapses several old categories. Is this a customer action, a third-party data access issue, a bot problem, a fraud problem, an authentication problem, or an AI governance problem?

The OCC’s focus on autonomy and financial harm is useful because it cuts through the naming debate. If a system can initiate or influence a financially meaningful action, the bank needs controls that match the blast radius.

## What should builders take from this?

The big product lesson is that “agent support” in financial services cannot just mean letting an LLM click through existing flows. Existing flows were designed around humans, sessions, and intent signals that are already fragile. Agents make those assumptions worse.

If you are building an AI product that touches bank accounts, personal finance, payments, lending, customer support, or identity, design for explicit permission boundaries from day one. Not one broad approval screen. Specific scopes. Short-lived access. Clear action previews. Human confirmation before irreversible steps. Audit logs that show what the agent saw, what it inferred, what it requested, and what the user approved.

If you are inside a bank, the same rule applies in reverse. Do not treat all AI traffic as one category. Separate internal assistants, customer-facing chat, vendor models, customer-authorized outside agents, and suspicious automated access. They have different owners and different failure modes.

My practitioner’s take: start by mapping every AI-assisted workflow against two simple questions, can it see sensitive data, and can it cause financial harm. If either answer is yes, the product needs tighter permissions, logging, and human checkpoints before it gets clever. The catch most teams miss is that the riskiest agent may not be the one they build. It may be the one their customer brings to the front door.
