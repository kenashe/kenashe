TL;DR: If your AI eval depends on fixed “personas,” it may be measuring a profile’s consistency, not how real people behave in context.

## What breaks when a persona stands in for a person?

The arXiv paper **“Personas Are Not People: Identity as State, Not Attribute”**, posted under cs.AI and cs.CL, goes after a common shortcut in AI evaluation: synthetic populations.

That means large sets of simulated people, each described with a profile. Age, job, attitudes, background, political lean, buying preferences, health beliefs, whatever the task needs. Then a language model is told to role-play those people. Teams use this to pretest surveys, simulate users, model communities, and stress-test AI systems.

The paper’s central claim is simple and uncomfortable: this treats identity like a fixed attribute. The same person-profile should act the same way across situations. But, according to the paper, three decades of personality research point the other way. People often vary more across contexts than people vary from one another.

That matters because most useful simulations are not asking trivia questions. They ask: will this community trust a clinical trial recruiter? Will this user click a scary privacy prompt? Will this customer complain, comply, churn, wait, or ask for help?

Those are context questions. A static persona can sound coherent and still be wrong.

## Does adding more detail fix synthetic populations?

The paper argues no. Not by itself.

“Personas Are Not People” separates behavior into three pieces: stable traits, the identity a context activates, and the situation itself. Drawing from identity economics, it frames people as carrying multiple possible identities and norms. Different settings call up different parts of the same person.

This is the part I think builders should sit with. The problem is not that the persona is too short. It is not only that the model needs more demographic fields, richer backstory, or more emotionally textured prompting. The paper says that, under its two stated assumptions, any fixed-persona simulation cannot reproduce the population behavior that is specific to that population and its circumstances.

That is a sharper claim than “LLMs are bad at role-play.” In fact, LLMs can be very good at role-play. That is part of the trap. The generated responses can feel plausible, varied, and human enough. But plausibility is not population validity.

The paper tests a person-in-context design on a naturalistic Reddit personality corpus and on a published clinical-trial recruitment survey involving a community with documented distrust of medical institutions. The design did create context-dependent behavior when tested directly. But it still did not fully match the real population in either setting.

Good. That is the right kind of result. Not “we solved synthetic users.” More like: here is a better abstraction, and here is where it still misses.

## What should builders do instead?

I would stop treating synthetic users as cheap replacements for users. Treat them as cheap generators of hypotheses.

A fixed persona can still help with product thinking. It can expose obvious UX gaps. It can pressure-test copy. It can help a team ask better interview questions before spending real research time. But once the question depends on trust, status, institutional history, incentives, group norms, fear, shame, or local context, the simulation needs grounding.

The better pattern is person-in-context: define not only who the person is, but what situation they believe they are in, what identity that situation activates, what norms are salient, and what real data will be used to check whether the synthetic behavior is even close.

The catch most teams miss is validation. If you cannot compare the synthetic population against real behavior from the population you care about, do not promote it to an eval gate. Use it as a sketchpad, not a scoreboard. For builders, I would try running your current persona eval twice: once with static profiles, once with explicit context and activated norms, then compare both against even a small slice of real user evidence. If the ranking changes, your “persona” was never the unit of behavior. The situation was.
