TL;DR: Hong Kong’s AI push against crypto crime will only work if banks treat agents as investigation copilots, not automated cops.

## What is Hong Kong actually asking banks to do?

The primary source here is Raymond Chan, Executive Director at the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, as reported by The Block: he said AI could help analyze both onchain transfers and offchain customer transactions to flag anomalies tied to crypto-related financial crime.

That sounds simple. It is not.

Onchain activity is public, messy, pseudonymous, and fast. Bank data is private, regulated, identity-linked, and full of normal weirdness. The useful signal lives in the join between the two. A wallet receives funds from a risky cluster. A customer suddenly sends money to an exchange. A pattern matches known scam cashout behavior. Or maybe it is a legitimate business, a payroll flow, a treasury move, or a customer trying to recover from a hacked wallet.

CoinDesk framed the same Hong Kong push as banks using AI to catch crypto crime before money disappears, while experts warned that detection has to happen without putting customer funds at risk. That is the real tension. Faster detection is good. Faster false positives can hurt real customers, trigger bad freezes, and create compliance theater.

This is where “agentic AI” is both interesting and overmarketed. An agent can pull data, compare patterns, ask for missing context, summarize an alert, and route a case. But if the system can also block transfers, freeze accounts, or escalate customers into enforcement queues without a strong human gate, the risk changes. Now it is not just analytics. It is operational power.

## Where would AI help, and where would it be dangerous?

The obvious win is triage.

Financial-crime teams already drown in alerts. Most are low value. AI can help rank cases, group related transactions, connect onchain addresses to offchain behavior, and write a plain-language case summary for an analyst. That is not glamorous. It is useful.

The harder job is anomaly detection across domains. A blockchain analytics tool might see exposure to a mixer, bridge, scam wallet, or sanctioned entity. A bank might see salary deposits, card spending, IP changes, device changes, beneficiary additions, and wire transfers. An AI system that can reason across those streams may catch patterns a rules engine misses.

But the weak point is explainability. If a bank cannot explain why a customer was flagged, the model becomes a liability. “The agent thought it looked suspicious” is not enough for compliance, customer support, or regulators. Banks need evidence trails: which transactions mattered, which behavior changed, which known typology it resembled, and which policy rule applies.

The other weak point is adversarial pressure. Crypto crime is adaptive. Once criminals learn the rough shape of a detection workflow, they route around it. They split flows, use intermediaries, mimic normal behavior, and test thresholds. AI can help update patterns, but it can also hallucinate connections or overweight noisy signals. In financial crime, confidence that feels persuasive is not the same as proof.

## What details are still missing?

The public reporting is thin on the parts that matter most. The Block reported Chan’s comments about using AI on onchain and offchain transactions. CoinDesk reported the broader Hong Kong effort and the expert concern about customer-fund risk. Neither account gives a specific model, vendor, benchmark, deployment timeline, error rate, governance standard, or appeal process.

Those omissions matter because “AI for crypto crime” can mean many things. A dashboard with better summaries. A machine-learning alert model. A retrieval system over case files. A chain-monitoring agent. A semi-autonomous workflow that takes action. Those are very different risk profiles.

My bias: banks should start with analyst augmentation, not automated enforcement. Use agents to gather context, reduce duplicate work, draft suspicious activity narratives, and surface why a case deserves attention. Keep freezes, exits, and law-enforcement referrals behind explicit human review. Measure false positives like a first-class harm, not a footnote.

For builders, the practical move is to design the audit trail before the agent. Connect the data sources, define allowed actions, log every tool call, require citations for each risk claim, and make the human approval step boring and mandatory. The catch most readers miss: the model is not the product here. The product is a defensible investigation workflow that can move faster without quietly turning uncertainty into punishment.
