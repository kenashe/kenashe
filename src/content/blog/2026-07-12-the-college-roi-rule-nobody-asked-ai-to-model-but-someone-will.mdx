A new federal rule says colleges have to leave graduates better off financially or risk losing access to federal financial aid. The bar, as reported on Hacker News, is roughly this: a program's graduates need to out-earn people who never went to college in the first place. If a program can't clear that line, the aid spigot gets threatened.

That's a policy story on its face. But it's also a data story, and the data part is where this gets interesting for anyone who builds. Because the second a rule ties money to a measurable outcome, someone has to measure it. Then someone has to predict it. Then someone has to game it, defend against gaming it, and audit the whole thing. Every one of those steps is now a place where models show up.

I want to walk through what that actually looks like, because "AI will help colleges comply" is the lazy version of this take. The real version is messier and more useful.

## The rule turns a fuzzy question into a scoreboard

For decades "is this degree worth it" was a dinner-table argument. Everyone had an opinion, nobody had a shared number. This rule changes that. It picks a comparison group (people who didn't attend college), picks an outcome (earnings), and draws a line.

The single detail I'd flag from the source is how narrow the framing is: better off, measured by earnings, versus a non-college baseline. That's a defensible choice and also a loaded one. Earnings are legible and trackable through tax and employment data. They're also a thin proxy for "better off." A nursing program and a social work program can both change lives and land on opposite sides of an earnings line. The rule doesn't care. The scoreboard doesn't have a column for that.

Once you have a scoreboard, you get everything scoreboards attract. Optimization, forecasting, and the quiet pressure to teach to the test. That's the part builders should sit with before they touch a keyboard.

## Where the models actually enter

Start with the most boring, most valuable job: prediction before enrollment. A college now has a strong incentive to know, in advance, which programs will clear the earnings bar and which won't. That's a supervised learning problem with genuinely good training data. Historical cohorts, program of study, financial aid amounts, and eventual earnings all exist and are increasingly linked through federal datasets.

A model that estimates expected post-graduation earnings by program isn't science fiction. Variations already exist in college-scorecard tooling. The rule just raises the stakes from "nice to know" to "keeps our funding." That changes who builds it and how carefully.

Then there's the harder job: attribution. Did the program raise earnings, or did it enroll people who were going to earn more anyway? This is the causal-inference problem hiding inside every ROI claim, and it's the one a naive earnings model gets wrong. A coding bootcamp that only admits people with CS-adjacent backgrounds will look fantastic on an earnings chart while adding little. A community college serving first-generation students may add enormous value and still land below the line because of where its students started.

If you build the naive version, you reward selection. If you build the honest version, you need counterfactuals, and counterfactuals are where machine learning gets slippery and where confident dashboards start lying.

## The gaming layer arrives on schedule

Any time an outcome controls money, the outcome gets managed. This is not cynicism, it's Goodhart's law, and it's been true of every metric-linked rule from hospital readmission rates to standardized testing.

For colleges, the moves are predictable. Shift enrollment toward students likely to earn more. Reclassify programs so weak ones get folded into strong ones. Push graduates toward higher-paying first jobs even if they're worse long-term fits, because the measurement window is what matters. Some of that is fine. Some of it quietly defeats the point of the rule, which was to protect students from debt that doesn't pay off.

Here's where I think AI plays a role people aren't talking about yet. The gaming will be too subtle and too high-volume for humans to catch manually. Detecting it will itself become a modeling problem. Regulators auditing compliance will need anomaly detection across thousands of programs: enrollment mix shifting suspiciously, earnings clustering right above the threshold, reclassifications timed to reporting periods. That's a fraud-detection shape, and it's a genuinely good use of the technology because the alternative is spot-checks and lawsuits.

So you get a two-sided arms race. Colleges model to optimize. Auditors model to catch optimization. The students the rule was meant to help are somewhere in the middle, hopefully benefiting from the friction.

## What a builder should not do here

The tempting product is a shiny "Program ROI Predictor" you sell to admissions offices. I'd be careful. A tool that helps a college decide who to admit based on predicted earnings is one lawsuit and one journalist away from being the story about how AI helped colleges cherry-pick students to protect federal funding. The intent might be compliance. The optics and the reality can both be ugly.

The better products live one layer over. Tools that help students understand real, program-specific earnings ranges before they take on debt. Tools that give regulators honest counterfactual estimates instead of raw averages. Tools that flag when a program's numbers are being managed rather than improved. Same underlying model, completely different alignment with who it serves.

## Practitioner's Take

If you want to build here, build the version that survives a Freedom of Information request and a hostile reporter. Start with the causal question, not the correlational one: don't predict earnings, predict earnings *lift* over a matched baseline, and be loud about your uncertainty. Use the federal earnings-and-aid linkage that this rule practically forces into existence as your training data, but treat selection bias as the main event, not a footnote. The catch most people will miss is that the easy model (average earnings by program) is worse than useless here, because it rewards colleges for admitting students who'd have succeeded anyway, which is the exact behavior the rule is trying to stop. If your model can't distinguish "this program helped" from "this program picked winners," you haven't built a compliance tool, you've built a laundering machine with a dashboard. Ship the honest version or don't ship.
