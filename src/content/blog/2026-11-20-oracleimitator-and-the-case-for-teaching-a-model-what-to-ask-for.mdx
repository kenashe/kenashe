TL;DR: A paper called "Active Feature Acquisition by Distilling an Oracle Teacher" shows that if you train a model to imitate a teacher that already knows the answer, it learns to ask for the right features in the right order, recovering 90% of the possible accuracy gain while spending under a quarter of the usual acquisition cost.

Most machine learning writing assumes the features are already there. You have the table, you have the columns, you train. But a lot of real decisions do not look like that. A doctor does not order every test at once. A fraud team does not pull every record on every transaction. Each measurement costs money, time, or a bit of someone's privacy. The real question is often not "what does the model predict" but "what should I go measure next, given what it already costs me to look."

That problem has a name: Active Feature Acquisition, or AFA. And the paper I want to talk about, "Active Feature Acquisition by Distilling an Oracle Teacher," posted to arXiv under both cs.AI and cs.LG, takes a sharp swing at how we train policies to solve it.

## What is active feature acquisition, and why is it hard?

Picture a model that starts with almost nothing. It can request feature one, or feature seven, or the expensive imaging scan. Each request has a price. It has a budget. After each acquisition it can decide to keep going or stop and predict. You want a policy that, instance by instance, picks the cheapest path to a confident answer.

The hard part is that this is sequential and conditional. The best thing to ask next depends on what you already know about this specific case. A test that is useless for one patient is decisive for another. So you cannot just rank features globally once and call it done. You need a policy that reasons per instance.

![a branching path where each node opens into a few possible next steps, with most branches thin and one glowing route cut](../../assets/blog/2026-11-20-oracleimitator-and-the-case-for-teaching-a-model-what-to-ask-for/inline-1.webp)

The way the field has mostly trained these policies, according to the paper, is by optimizing a self-constructed utility target. In plain terms: the policy invents its own notion of how useful each acquisition would be and chases that. It is learning a moving target it defined for itself. That works, but it is indirect, and it is noisy, because the thing you are imitating is a guess about value rather than value itself.

## How does distilling an oracle teacher change the setup?

Here is the move that makes this paper interesting. At training time, you actually have everything. You know the label. You know the feature values the deployed model will never get to see for free. So the authors build an explicit teacher out of that privileged information.

They call it an oracle. Because it can see the label and the hidden feature values, it can compute, in closed form, how much each candidate feature would reduce the downstream predictor's error. It ranks every possible next acquisition by its true effect on accuracy. Not an estimate. The actual ranking, given information the student will not have.

Then OracleImitator, the method, distills that ranking into a deployable policy by soft-target imitation. The student watches the oracle's ranking and learns to reproduce it using only what is visible at deployment. Classic teacher-student distillation, but the teacher is not a bigger neural net. It is an algorithmic object you can write down.

![a figure with full sight guiding a blindfolded figure along the same path, the two walking in parallel](../../assets/blog/2026-11-20-oracleimitator-and-the-case-for-teaching-a-model-what-to-ask-for/inline-2.webp)

That distinction matters more than it sounds. When your teacher is a large model, distillation is a black box compressing a black box. When your teacher is an explicit closed-form oracle, you know exactly what the student is trying to copy. You can ask a question most distillation work cannot: how much of the teacher's ranking is even recoverable from what the student can see at deployment? The paper says you can measure that directly, because the teacher is not an implicit optimization target buried in a loss function. It is a defined thing.

That is the part I keep coming back to. The method is good, but the diagnostic that falls out of it is the quieter win.

## Do the numbers hold up?

The reported results are the kind I like because they are about cost, not just accuracy. Across multimodal medical diagnosis and the AFABench benchmark, OracleImitator matches or outperforms existing AFA methods across the budget range. The headline figure: recovering 90% of the attainable accuracy gain at under a quarter of the acquisition cost.

Read that carefully. It is not "90% accuracy." It is 90% of the gain that was possible to get from acquiring features, at less than 25% of the spend. The ceiling here is what you could achieve by acquiring everything. The point is how close you get to that ceiling while buying far fewer measurements.

I want to flag what the paper does not settle, because an honest read demands it. Both arXiv listings carry identical abstracts, so there is one result set here, not two independent confirmations. The claims are the authors' own, on their own benchmark selection plus AFABench. "Matches or outperforms across the budget range" is a strong phrasing that I would want to see broken out curve by curve before treating it as a clean win at every budget. And the oracle depends on having the label and hidden features at training time, which you do in a historical dataset but which assumes your training distribution matches deployment. If the cost of a feature or the population shifts, the distilled policy is imitating a teacher that was right about a world that moved.

## What should a builder actually do with this?

If you are shipping anything where inputs cost money, start by writing down the price of each feature. Most teams skip this. They treat all columns as free because the training CSV is already paid for. The whole premise of AFA is that deployment is where the bill lands, and you cannot optimize a cost you never wrote down.

Then try the oracle trick even if you never deploy OracleImitator. In your historical data you have the labels and the full feature set. You can compute, retrospectively, which features actually moved predictions for which cases. That ranking alone is a map: it tells you where your expensive measurements are earning their keep and where you are paying for columns that rarely change the answer. You might find a cheaper fixed acquisition order that captures most of the value, which is simpler to ship than a learned per-instance policy.

The catch most readers will miss: the method's elegance is also its constraint. The oracle is only honest when training-time privileged information genuinely reflects deployment. In medicine, where the paper tests, that is plausible because physiology does not shift monthly. In fraud, pricing, or anything adversarial, the cost structure and the signal both move, and a policy distilled from a frozen oracle will confidently ask for the wrong thing at the wrong price. Use this where the world holds still long enough for the teacher to still be right.
