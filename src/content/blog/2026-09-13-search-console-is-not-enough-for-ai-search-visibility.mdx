TL;DR: Search Console can still help, but it is no longer a complete map of search visibility when AI answers can surface, summarize, or bury pages in ways the reporting does not clearly show.

## What did Google admit about AI search reporting?

Search Engine Journal’s report, “Google Admits Search Console Reporting For AI Search Is Inadequate” via Roger Montti, says Google has acknowledged that Search Console does not adequately reflect AI search positioning data for SEO.

That is the whole practical problem.

For years, Search Console has been the closest thing most site owners had to an official search visibility dashboard. It was never perfect. It was sampled, delayed, and often frustrating. But it gave operators a shared scoreboard. Queries. Pages. Movement. Drops. Recovery signs.

AI search breaks that mental model.

A page can matter without getting a clean blue-link style position. It can be used as background. It can be cited. It can be ignored while a competitor gets named. It can support an answer without earning the click. Or it can rank in normal results and still lose attention because an AI-generated response satisfies the user first.

If Search Console cannot show that layer clearly, SEO teams are flying with an instrument panel built for a different aircraft.

That does not mean Search Console is useless. It means the old habit of saying “position went up, therefore visibility improved” is weaker than it used to be.

## What should SEOs measure instead?

The wrong reaction is to invent fake precision.

I would not trust a vendor that claims it can perfectly measure “AI search share” across every user, device, query rewrite, and generated answer. AI results can vary. They can change with context. They can be personalized or regenerated. Even when the same topic is searched twice, the answer surface may not behave like a stable ranked list.

The useful reaction is simpler: build your own directional layer.

Track a small set of high-intent queries by hand or with a repeatable workflow. Capture what the AI answer says. Note whether your brand or page is cited, mentioned, or absent. Compare that against traditional ranking and traffic. Over time, the interesting cases are mismatches: ranking well but absent from AI answers, cited often but not clicked, or losing citations after content changes.

That gives you a working diagnosis, not a fantasy metric.

Ashe runs Lucky Domains, which works on SEO and search visibility for sites that need to rank, and this is exactly where the reporting gap matters: decisions about content, architecture, and authority still have to be made even when Google’s own dashboard is incomplete.

## Does this change the content strategy?

Yes, but not in the lazy “write for AI” way.

The basics still matter: clear pages, strong topical coverage, original evidence, crawlable structure, and content that answers real questions better than the next result. But AI search adds pressure to make pages easier to extract from and trust.

That means fewer vague intros. Better definitions. Cleaner comparisons. More first-party data where you have it. Clear authorship when expertise matters. Pages that answer the specific sub-question, not just the broad keyword.

It also means separating two jobs that used to be mashed together.

One job is ranking. The other is being selected as useful context for an answer. They overlap, but they are not identical. If Search Console cannot fully show the second job, teams need their own checks.

Practitioner’s Take: Pick 20 to 50 queries that actually matter to the business, not vanity head terms. Once a week, record the normal search result, the AI answer behavior, who gets mentioned, who gets cited, and whether your page appears in either surface. Then update pages based on the misses you can explain. The catch most readers miss: this is not about replacing Search Console. It is about treating Search Console as one signal, not the whole truth.
