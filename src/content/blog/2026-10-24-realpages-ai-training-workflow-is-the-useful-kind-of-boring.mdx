TL;DR: RealPage’s ChatGPT workflow is a good reminder that the first high-value enterprise AI use case is often not automation, it is making internal training faster, more specific, and easier to keep current.

## What is RealPage actually doing with ChatGPT?

In OpenAI’s "How Realpage. builds AI training with ChatGPT Work," Austin Bram, director of AI enablement at RealPage, describes a very normal, very useful workflow.

His job is to help RealPage team members understand how to use AI in practical ways that improve daily work. That means creating training materials for different teams, different roles, and different skill levels. Bram says he uses ChatGPT Enterprise to take his ideas, notes, and source materials, then turn them into full enablement packages.

That is the whole story. No autonomous agent swarm. No claim that the company replaced a department. No giant benchmark chart. Just a person responsible for internal AI adoption using ChatGPT to package knowledge faster.

I like this example because it matches how AI actually enters companies. Not as one magic product. As a layer inside work that was already happening.

Training is a good fit because it has three traits AI handles well: messy inputs, repeatable structure, and lots of audience variation. One group needs a high-level intro. Another needs hands-on examples. Another needs guardrails. A new hire needs context. A manager needs a decision framework. The same source material can become many versions, if someone competent is steering it.

## Why does this matter more than another chatbot demo?

Most enterprise AI programs do not fail because the model cannot write. They fail because people do not know what to ask, where the boundaries are, or how the tool fits their real work.

That makes enablement a bottleneck. And bottlenecks are where AI can be useful.

The RealPage example points to a practical operating model: collect the rough material from the business, use AI to draft role-specific training assets, then have the enablement lead edit, contextualize, and publish. The human is not removed. The human is amplified in the least glamorous part of the job: turning scattered knowledge into something colleagues can use.

There is also a subtle point here. Bram is not describing generic “AI literacy” content. He is building materials for teams and roles. That matters. Generic training teaches people what AI is. Role-based training teaches them what to do on Monday.

The difference is adoption.

A support manager does not need the same package as a sales operations analyst. A legal reviewer does not need the same examples as a product marketer. Skill level matters too. Beginners need confidence and constraints. Advanced users need patterns, failure modes, and review habits.

If ChatGPT helps one enablement person produce those variants without starting from scratch each time, that is real operating leverage, even if nobody puts it on a keynote slide.

## What is missing from the OpenAI and RealPage example?

The clip does not provide hard outcomes. OpenAI does not report time saved, course completion rates, quality scores, adoption lift, customer impact, or error reduction in the transcript provided. So I would not treat this as proof that ChatGPT Enterprise guarantees better training.

It is better understood as a workflow pattern.

The risk is also obvious: AI can make mediocre training faster. If the inputs are vague, the policy guidance is stale, or the reviewer is not close to the work, the output may look polished while teaching the wrong thing. That is worse than a rough internal doc, because polish lowers skepticism.

The check is to make the workflow evidence-based. Use approved source material. Preserve links back to internal policy and product docs. Ask subject-matter experts to review examples. Test the training with a small group before rolling it out. Track whether people can actually perform the task after taking it.

Also, keep a library of prompts and package formats. Not because prompts are magic, but because consistency helps. A useful enablement machine should produce recognizable artifacts: quick-start guides, scenario exercises, manager talking points, risk checklists, and before-and-after examples.

If I were building this inside a company, I would start with one role, one workflow, and one measurable behavior. Pick something people already struggle with, like writing better customer follow-ups, summarizing account history, or drafting internal status updates. Feed ChatGPT the approved material, generate a training pack, have the domain owner review it, then run it with 10 users and watch where they still get stuck. The catch most teams miss: the model can draft the curriculum, but only the business can decide what “good work” looks like.
