TL;DR: If you are building a consumer AI product, the name and domain are not decoration, they are part of distribution, trust, and category positioning from day one.

## What actually happened with Dark.com?

The primary source here is DomainInvesting’s “Deepinder Goyal Acquires Another Valuable Domain Name.” DomainInvesting reported that Deepinder Goyal, founder of Zomato and Eternal, has added Dark.com to his domain portfolio, and said it is “safe to say” the name was acquired for Dark, the AI chat platform being developed by Eternal.

That wording matters. This is not a first-party announcement from Eternal in the material provided. DomainInvesting also reported that Goyal had posted on LinkedIn three weeks earlier that Dark would be found at Dark.ai. So the visible sequence is: Dark.ai is announced as the home for the product, then Dark.com appears to be acquired.

That is a familiar move. Launch on the AI-native extension, then secure the.com if the product is serious enough, visible enough, or likely to be confused with something else.

It does not tell us whether Dark will win users. It does not tell us the product quality, model stack, retention, or go-to-market. It does tell us that the team appears to be treating the brand as a strategic asset, not a placeholder.

## Why would an AI chat product care this much about the.com?

Consumer AI is getting crowded. Chat products are especially hard to differentiate at the surface level. A landing page promises intelligence. A text box waits for input. A few sample prompts sit underneath. Most users cannot tell, at first glance, whether the magic is model quality, workflow design, memory, integrations, pricing, or just a clean demo.

In that environment, names do work.

A short, exact.com reduces ambiguity. It is easier to say out loud. Easier to remember after seeing it once. Easier to trust when someone hears about it in a group chat, on a podcast, or from a colleague. It also prevents a common failure mode: a product spends money creating demand, then some of that demand leaks to the owner of the matching.com.

That does not mean every AI startup needs the perfect domain. Most do not. Plenty of strong products have launched from imperfect names. But there is a difference between a tool for developers that spreads through GitHub and Discord, and a consumer AI brand that needs broad recall.

Dark is also an interesting word for an AI chat product. It is short, loaded, and a little risky. It can signal mystery, privacy, depth, or edge. It can also signal opacity. That makes the domain even more important. If you choose a word with emotional weight, you probably want to control the canonical address.

## What should builders take from this?

I run [Lucky Domains](https://luckydomains.io/how-we-buy-domains.html), which buys names that are not listed for sale, and the part outsiders often miss is that domain strategy is usually about timing and risk, not vanity.

If you are testing a prototype, use the available name and ship. Do not let naming become a procrastination machine. But once a product starts showing signs of real pull, the cost of cleaning up the brand later can rise quickly. Not just in money. In redirects, user confusion, support issues, app store naming, trademark review, press references, and the quiet drag of always explaining where the product lives.

The practical move is simple: decide what name tier matches the stage. For a weekend project, good enough is good enough. For a funded consumer AI product, map the domain landscape before the launch story hardens. Who owns the.com? Is it active? Is there an obvious conflict? Are users likely to type the wrong address? Can you live with that if the product works?

My take: Dark.com is not the story because it proves anything about Eternal’s AI product. It is the story because it shows a serious consumer AI operator treating distribution as more than model access. The catch most builders miss is that brand debt compounds quietly. You can ignore it early, but if the product hits, the market will force the cleanup later.
