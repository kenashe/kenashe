TL;DR: More Google searches do not automatically mean more publisher traffic, because AI answers can satisfy demand inside the results page before a click ever happens.

## What does it mean when queries rise but revenue does not follow?

Search Engine Journal’s piece, “Google Search Queries Hit A 5-Year High, And Search Revenue Didn’t Follow” by Duane Forrester, reports a strange split: Google search queries reached a five-year high, while search revenue grew 12%.

That is not a collapse. A 12% revenue increase is still a very large business moving up. But the mismatch matters because search has usually been easy to reason about: more searches, more ads, more clicks, more value moving through the system.

AI Mode makes that chain messier.

Forrester’s read is that the gap points to AI Mode, where Google can answer more directly inside the search experience. If users get what they need there, the query still counts. The attention still exists. But the downstream click may not. Google’s reported fix, per Search Engine Journal, is more ads inside the answer.

That is the operating shift. Search is no longer just a router. It is becoming a destination, answer engine, ad surface, shopping assistant, and research layer all at once.

## Is this bad for SEO?

Bad for lazy SEO, yes.

If your traffic model depends on ranking for simple informational queries that an AI answer can summarize in four sentences, this is pressure. Definitions, basic comparisons, short how-tos, and commodity listicles are easier to compress into the results page.

But I would not reduce this to “SEO is dead.” That line has been wrong for 20 years, mostly because people keep confusing the tactic with the underlying job.

The job is still demand capture. People still search when they have intent. They still need vendors, products, local services, expert judgment, original data, calculators, community, proof, and trust. AI answers change where the first answer appears. They do not remove the need for the deeper page when the stakes are higher.

## What should operators change now?

First, separate query visibility from click value. A page can still be discovered, cited, summarized, or used as context without producing the same click behavior it did before. That means old reporting will under-explain what is happening. Rankings alone are weaker. Raw traffic alone is weaker. You need to look at qualified visits, branded search lift, assisted conversions, email capture, demo requests, quote requests, and repeat visitors.

Second, build pages that cannot be fully consumed as snippets. This does not mean hiding the answer. It means adding things AI summaries are bad at replacing: original examples, workflows, screenshots, templates, named experience, product constraints, before-and-after comparisons, and clear next steps.

Third, watch ad placement inside AI answers with a skeptical eye. If Google adds more ads into answer surfaces, advertisers may follow, but publishers may still see fewer organic exits. That is not automatically unfair, and it is not automatically good. It is a market design problem. The company controlling the answer box also controls the ad inventory around that answer.

My practical move: audit your top 50 organic pages and mark which ones are “answerable in-place” versus “click-worthy after the answer.” Rewrite the first group into stronger second-click assets, tools, comparisons, examples, or conversion pages. The catch most teams miss: AI search does not only threaten traffic, it changes what a useful page is.
