TL;DR: The best streaming video model tested on FastBench scores 50.7% on fast real-world events, which means live video agents still miss the fast stuff, and the fix (sample more frames) runs into a context budget wall fast.

Most video benchmarks have been testing the easy case. A person talks to a camera. A slideshow advances. A cooking demo moves at human pace. Models look good on those because nothing important happens between one second and the next. FastBench is built to break that comfort.

## What is FastBench actually testing?

The paper is **FastBench: Can Streaming VLMs Perceive High-Dynamic Real-World Streams?**, posted to arXiv cs.CL. The question in the title is the whole point. Streaming video language models process video as a continuous feed instead of a fixed clip, which is what you need for anything live: a robot watching its workspace, an agent monitoring a security feed, a model calling plays off a sports broadcast.

The problem is a budget fight. A model has a bounded context, and inside that budget it has to spend tokens on three things at once: how much history to keep, how sharp each frame is, and how many frames per second it samples. Push one up and another drops. Sample at 1 to 2 FPS (common practice) and you save budget, but you blow past fast events entirely. A ball changes direction. A hand swaps tools. A car turns. At 2 FPS those moments fall into the gap between samples.

FastBench is deliberately small and clean: 306 QA pairs across eight domains, six capability types, and three temporal scopes the authors call forward, instant, and backward (did something happen next, now, or before). What makes it credible is the construction, not the size.

## How do they know the questions are fair?

This is where most video benchmarks get sloppy, and where FastBench earns attention. The pipeline generates questions from high-FPS clips, then filters out anything answerable at 2 FPS. That filter is the clever part. If a question can be answered from sparse sampling, it does not test fast perception, so it gets cut. What survives genuinely requires seeing the fast action.

Answers are verified with object trajectories using SAM3 and CoTracker3, so a "what happened" answer is grounded in where things actually moved, not in a model's guess about plausible motion. Then three rounds of human inspection on top. They also hand-annotate evidence intervals, meaning each question comes with the exact window where the answer lives. That is the kind of ground truth that lets you measure whether a model looked at the right moment, not just whether it got lucky.

For anyone who builds evals, this is the template. Generate hard, filter for the capability you actually care about, verify with an independent signal, then put humans on it. The filtering step especially. A benchmark that includes questions your baseline sampling can already answer is measuring the wrong thing and inflating scores.

## Why does the best model still only hit 50.7%?

The headline number: Gemini-3.5-Flash, the strongest model tested, scores 50.7%. On a benchmark built so you have to see fast events, the best available model is near coin-flip territory. That gap is the story.

The scaling behavior underneath is more useful than the single score. Qwen3-VL-8B goes from 32.9% at 2 FPS to 44.6% at 24 FPS. So denser sampling clearly helps, a 12-point jump. But the authors note the gains saturate as history gets compressed. You feed in more frames, the context budget fills, older history gets crushed into a sparse summary, and the benefit flattens. More FPS is not free. You pay for it by forgetting the past.

That is the real constraint builders need to sit with. You cannot just crank the frame rate and call it solved. There is a ceiling where added temporal detail starts eating the memory the model needs to reason about what came before.

## Can a model decide for itself when to look closer?

The authors propose a training-free baseline called ProactiveFrame. The idea is sensible: use a dual-tier sliding window that keeps recent observations at high FPS while downsampling older frames into sparse history, and adjust the incoming frame rate through text tokens. Recent and fast, old and cheap.

It works, modestly. ProactiveFrame beats sparse uniform sampling by 5.4 and 1.5 percentage points depending on the setup. Real gains, not noise. But it stays well below what the paper calls oracle-guided focusing, which is the score you get when the model is told exactly where to look. That gap between ProactiveFrame and the oracle is the honest punchline: current models cannot reliably tell, from the stream alone, when they need to look harder. They do not know what they are about to miss.

That is a harder problem than sampling. It is a metacognition problem. The model has to predict that something fast is coming and spend budget before it sees the payoff. Humans do this constantly when we watch sports or drive. These models do not yet.

## What a builder should take from this

If you are shipping anything that watches a live feed, treat frame rate as a tunable you measure, not a default you accept. The Qwen numbers say the jump from 2 FPS to higher rates is worth real points, so test your own task across sampling rates before you assume the model is the bottleneck. It might just be starving for frames.

Second, build the budget tradeoff into your design from the start. The saturation finding means there is a point where more FPS stops paying and starts costing you memory. Find that point on your workload. A dual-tier window like ProactiveFrame (recent at high rate, old at low rate) is a cheap pattern to copy and needs no training.

The catch most readers will miss: the oracle gap, not the 50.7% headline, is the thing that blocks production. You can brute-force a bit more accuracy with denser sampling, but you cannot brute-force a model knowing when to pay attention. Until a streaming VLM can flag its own uncertainty and ask for more detail on the fly, live video agents need a cheaper trigger outside the model (motion detection, event cues, a lightweight detector) to tell the expensive model when the fast part is coming. Build that trigger. Do not wait for the model to grow it.

Code and data are at the project's GitHub, which makes this one of the easier benchmarks to actually run against your own pipeline rather than take on faith.
