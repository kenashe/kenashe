Everyone selling coding agents wants you to picture a firehose: agents cranking out pull requests around the clock, humans skimming and merging, throughput up 10x. A new arXiv paper looked at what's actually landing in real repositories, and the picture is quieter than the pitch.

Researchers analyzed 25,264 agentic pull requests from 2,361 popular GitHub repositories over a three-month window. The headline number sounds big. The distribution behind it is the real story. The median repository produced just one to two agentic PRs across the entire three months. Not per day. Not per week. Total.

So adoption is happening. But intensive adoption, the kind that changes how a team ships, is concentrated in a small slice of projects. Most repos are dabbling.

## The median repo is barely using this

One to two PRs in three months is a trial, not a workflow. It's the "let me point Copilot's agent at one issue and see what happens" behavior. The study makes clear that the aggregate number is dragged up by a handful of heavy users, not spread evenly across the population.

This matters because the marketing math almost always runs the other direction. Vendors cite an industry estimate of 36 PRs per participant over a similar period as a kind of productivity benchmark. The paper found that a small number of projects clear that bar. Most do not. The gap between "a few power users hit 36" and "the median hits 1 to 2" is the gap between a case study and a norm.

I read this as a healthy correction, not a disappointment. New tools always show a long tail before they show a fat middle. The question is which projects are in the fat right side of the curve, and why.

## Small projects are punching above their weight

Here's the finding I didn't expect. Projects with 1 to 5 contributors showed higher participation ratios and higher average agentic PR activity than medium or large projects.

That inverts the usual enterprise story, where the big org with the big budget adopts first. In agentic coding, the small team moves faster. It makes sense once you think about the friction. A solo maintainer or a three-person project has no review board, no CODEOWNERS gauntlet, no compliance sign-off. One person decides to try an agent, points it at the backlog, and merges what looks right. The decision loop is one human long.

Large projects carry the opposite weight. More contributors means more people who need to agree that agent output is acceptable, more conventions the agent has to respect, and more reviewers who might bounce a PR on style alone. The tooling scales. The social process doesn't, at least not yet.

If you're a vendor betting on top-down enterprise rollouts, this is a warning. The early traction is bottoms-up, in the places with the least governance overhead.

## Oversight is one human, every time

The most consistent pattern in the data is the shape of the collaboration. Human-agent work is dominated by a single-human oversight model: one developer reviews or modifies the agent's contribution, and that's the whole loop. Multi-human collaboration around an agent, where two or more people co-manage or co-review its work, remains uncommon.

This is quietly important. It means the current unit of agentic coding is one person plus one agent, not a team plus a fleet. The agent isn't a new team member that everyone interacts with. It's a power tool that belongs to one owner, who is accountable for what it ships.

![a single human figure standing between a machine and a repository, acting as the only gate](../../assets/blog/2026-07-16-what-25-264-agentic-pull-requests-reveal-about-real-adoption/inline-2.png)

That single owner is a bottleneck and a safeguard at the same time. Safeguard, because someone is always on the hook for the merge. Bottleneck, because the whole approach is capped by how much one person can review. You can generate 100 agentic PRs, but if one human has to vet each one, your throughput is that human's attention, not the agent's speed.

Which brings the productivity story back down to earth. The constraint on agentic coding right now is not model capability. It's review bandwidth. The paper says this directly: successful integration depends not just on agent capability but on the human and organizational processes governing their use. The agents got good faster than the org charts adapted.

## What the study can't tell us yet

Worth keeping the frame honest. This is an early snapshot, and the authors say so. Three months. Popular repos, which skews toward projects with active maintainers and existing tooling. And PRs are a visible, countable proxy for adoption, but they miss the agentic work that never becomes a PR: the local coding sessions, the throwaway scripts, the refactors a developer runs with an agent and commits directly without the PR ceremony.

So the true adoption number is almost certainly higher than what PR counts show. The paper measures the formal, reviewed, merged-into-a-shared-repo slice. A lot of agent usage lives outside that slice. The single-human oversight finding is robust for the PR pathway, but it says less about the solo developer using an agent all day in a private branch.

Two datapoints I'd want next: churn on agentic PRs versus human ones, and time-to-merge. If agent PRs get reverted more or sit longer in review, the raw count overstates real value.

## The practitioner's take

If you run a team, stop benchmarking yourself against the 36-PRs-per-person number. That's the ceiling a few tuned projects hit, not a target for month one. The realistic near-term move is to pick one owner per repo, give them the agent, and let them build the review muscle before you scale bodies at it.

The lever this study exposes is review bandwidth, not generation speed. So the highest-value thing you can do is not "add more agents." It's make review cheaper: tighten your test coverage so an agent PR either passes CI or doesn't, write explicit contribution conventions the agent can follow, and scope agent tasks small enough that one human can verify a diff in minutes, not hours. Small, well-tested projects are winning early precisely because those things are easy for them.

The catch most people miss: the bottleneck moves, it doesn't vanish. Speed up generation and you flood the reviewer. Speed up review and you'll need better signals about which PRs to trust without reading every line. The team that wins the next year isn't the one with the best coding agent. It's the one that figures out how to let more than one human, or one human plus good automated checks, safely govern a growing pile of agent output.
