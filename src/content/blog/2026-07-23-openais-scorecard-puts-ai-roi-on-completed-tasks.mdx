TL;DR: OpenAI’s proposed AI scorecard is a good operating frame because it measures AI by successful work, not model novelty or usage volume.

## What should an AI scorecard actually measure?

Sarah Friar, CFO of OpenAI, laid out “A scorecard for the AI age,” a practical framework for measuring AI ROI across four buckets: useful work, cost per successful task, dependability, and return on compute.

That is the right direction.

Most AI measurement still gets stuck in weak proxies. Number of users. Number of prompts. Tokens consumed. Minutes saved, self-reported after a pilot. Those can be useful signals, but they are not the thing. The thing is whether a workflow produces an acceptable result at an acceptable cost, often enough to trust it in production.

“Useful work” is the anchor. Not activity. Not engagement. Work. Did the claims review get completed? Did the sales research package meet the rep’s standard? Did the support agent resolve the issue without escalation? Did the analyst get a usable first draft that survived human review?

The second measure, cost per successful task, is where the scorecard gets sharper. AI teams often talk about cost per token or cost per API call because those are easy to pull from a dashboard. Operators need the blended cost of a finished unit of work. That includes retries, failed generations, human review, tool calls, latency costs, and maintenance.

If an AI system costs pennies per call but needs five attempts and a human cleanup pass, the headline price is noise.

## Why dependability matters more than peak capability

Friar’s inclusion of dependability is the most important part of the scorecard. It is also the part teams are most tempted to hand-wave.

A model that performs brilliantly 80% of the time can still be useless for the wrong workflow. A model that performs modestly but predictably may be easier to ship. The question is not “Can it do the task?” The question is “Can it do the task under normal operating conditions, with normal messy inputs, without surprising the humans who depend on it?”

This is where demos lie. Demos show ceiling. Operations expose variance.

Dependability needs to be measured at the task level, not the model level. A customer support workflow might need high factual accuracy, low escalation risk, and consistent tone. A coding assistant might tolerate some wrong suggestions if the developer remains in the loop. A finance process may require strict citation and audit trails. Same model, different success bar.

Return on compute is also a useful CFO-level addition. It forces a question many teams avoid: are we spending scarce compute on the highest-value work? That applies whether the compute is rented through an API, bought as GPUs, or buried inside an enterprise contract. Compute is not just an engineering concern. It is now an allocation decision.

## Where the scorecard can go wrong

The danger is turning the scorecard into theater.

“Successful task” sounds clean until a team has to define it. If the definition is loose, every pilot looks good. If the definition is too strict, teams may kill useful systems before they improve. The trick is to set a threshold that matches the workflow’s real risk.

For low-risk internal drafting, success might mean “good enough for a human to edit in half the usual time.” For a compliance workflow, success might mean “passes review with citations and no unsupported claims.” For an agent that can take actions in software, success should include whether it avoided harmful or irreversible steps.

Another trap is measuring AI in isolation. The better comparison is against the current process. Humans are not perfect baselines. Existing workflows have error rates, delays, rework, and hidden labor. A fair scorecard compares the AI-assisted process to the old process across quality, time, cost, and risk.

I also want teams to track learning rate. Not as a vanity metric, but as an operating signal. If cost per successful task drops every week because prompts, evals, routing, and tools are improving, that system may be worth continuing. If it stays flat after the easy fixes, the workflow may not be ready.

Practitioner’s Take: Pick one workflow and define one finished unit of work. Then measure three numbers for two weeks: success rate, total cost per success, and human minutes per success. Do not start with a giant AI ROI dashboard. Start with one task where the baseline is known and the review standard is clear. The catch most teams miss: the eval is the product. Without a real success definition, the scorecard becomes another slide about “AI transformation” instead of a way to decide what to ship.
