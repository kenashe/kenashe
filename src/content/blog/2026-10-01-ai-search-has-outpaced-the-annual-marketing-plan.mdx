TL;DR: AI-assisted buyers now research, compare, and shortlist faster than most companies can update their marketing assumptions, so planning needs shorter feedback loops, not just more AI-generated content.

## What actually changed about the buyer?

Duane Forrester’s Search Engine Journal piece, “AI Changed Your Buyer Faster Than Your Business Can Adapt,” makes a simple claim with teeth: AI changed the buyer in 18 months, while many businesses still plan around 12-month cycles.

That gap matters because AI did not only add another channel. It changed the shape of discovery.

A buyer can now ask for a shortlist, compare vendors, summarize reviews, pressure-test claims, draft an RFP, and identify likely objections before ever touching your site. The old model assumed a company could educate the market through its own pages, ads, email, webinars, and sales calls. That still happens. But more of the buyer’s early thinking is now mediated by answer engines, chat interfaces, review summaries, analyst snippets, community posts, and whatever an AI system can scrape, retrieve, or infer.

The dangerous mistake is treating this as an SEO formatting problem. Yes, pages still need to be crawlable, clear, and useful. But the bigger issue is whether your market story survives when it is removed from your navigation, stripped of your design, summarized next to competitors, and questioned by a buyer who can generate ten follow-up questions in ten seconds.

## Why do annual plans break here?

Annual planning assumes the market will not move too far before the next reset. AI buyer behavior makes that assumption shakier.

A 12-month content calendar can lock a team into messages that were plausible when they were approved but stale by the time they ship. Quarterly SEO targets can miss the fact that buyers are asking different questions now. Sales enablement can lag behind the objections prospects are learning from AI tools before the first call.

Forrester’s framing is useful because it points at operating tempo, not just technology adoption. The companies that adapt fastest will not be the ones that publish the most AI-written pages. They will be the ones that can see the buyer’s new questions early and change the business response quickly.

That means search data, sales call notes, support tickets, community chatter, paid search terms, and AI answer monitoring should not sit in separate silos. They are all signals from the same buyer. If marketing sees one version, sales sees another, and product hears a third, the company gets slower exactly when the buyer gets faster.

Ashe runs Lucky Domains, which works on [SEO and search visibility](https://luckydomains.io/services.html#seo), and this is where I see the real shift: ranking is still valuable, but the job is increasingly to make a business legible across many surfaces that may summarize it without permission or context.

## What should teams change this quarter?

Start with the questions buyers are asking now. Not the personas in last year’s deck. The actual questions showing up in search queries, sales calls, chat logs, Reddit threads, LinkedIn comments, customer emails, review sites, and AI-generated comparison prompts.

Then map those questions to assets. If a buyer asks “Is this safe for a regulated team?” and your only answer is a vague security page, that is a gap. If a buyer asks “How does this compare to the cheaper tool?” and your sales team handles it live every week but your site says nothing concrete, that is a gap. If AI systems keep flattening your product into the wrong category, that is a positioning gap.

The fix is not to spin up a giant AI content machine. That usually creates more surface area with less trust. The better move is a monthly buyer-intelligence loop: collect the new questions, identify where current assets fail, update the pages and sales materials that matter, then check whether the market response changes.

For practitioners, I would run a 30-day audit with three inputs: ten real sales conversations, twenty high-intent search queries, and a set of AI answers to comparison and problem-aware prompts in your category. Look for mismatches between what buyers now believe and what your business still says. Update the few pages that influence shortlists first. The catch most teams miss: AI did not make strategy obsolete. It made stale strategy visible faster.
