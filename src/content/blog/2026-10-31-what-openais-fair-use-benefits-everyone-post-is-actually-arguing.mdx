TL;DR: OpenAI's "Fair use benefits everyone" post is a curated set of supporting court briefs, not a legal ruling, so read it as an advocacy brief about who benefits from training on copyrighted data, not as settled law.

The primary source here is a single OpenAI blog post titled "Fair use benefits everyone," which collects excerpts from amicus and supporting briefs filed by creators, academics, accessibility advocates, and legal experts. That's the whole artifact. No model release, no benchmark, no new product. It's a policy move dressed in the language of consensus, and it's worth reading carefully precisely because it reads so smoothly.

I'll say the obvious part first: OpenAI has an enormous stake in how courts interpret fair use for model training. A ruling that training on copyrighted works without a license is infringement would reshape its cost structure and possibly its existing models. So when OpenAI publishes a page of voices agreeing with its position, the selection is the argument. That doesn't make the briefs wrong. It means the post is evidence of who OpenAI could find to agree, not a neutral survey of where the law stands.

## What is OpenAI actually claiming here?

The core claim, stated in the title, is that fair use benefits everyone, not just AI labs. The post assembles briefs grouped by constituency: working creators, academic researchers, accessibility advocates, and legal scholars. The framing is that fair use is a longstanding doctrine that has historically enabled new technologies and new forms of expression, and that AI training fits that tradition.

![a wide set of distinct voices converging toward a single doorway](../../assets/blog/2026-10-31-what-openais-fair-use-benefits-everyone-post-is-actually-arguing/inline-1.webp)

That's a real legal tradition. Fair use has protected search engines indexing the web, reverse engineering for interoperability, and transformative uses of existing works. The strongest version of OpenAI's argument is that training a model on text to learn statistical patterns is transformative in the same way a search index is: you're not redistributing the works, you're building something that serves a different function.

The weaker version, and the one the blog format encourages, is a vibe. Lots of sympathetic people agree, therefore the law should too. Accessibility advocates benefiting from AI tools is genuinely important. It is also not a fair use holding. Courts weigh four statutory factors, and "this technology helps blind users" is relevant to some of them but doesn't resolve the question of whether ingesting copyrighted books to train a commercial model is fair. OpenAI knows this. The post is built to blur the line between "these uses are good" and "therefore training is legal."

## Who signed these briefs, and does it matter?

The post attributes excerpts to creators, academics, accessibility advocates, and legal experts. I'd treat the specific identities and exact wording as reported by OpenAI itself, because that's the only source here. The blog is selecting and excerpting, so we're seeing the parts OpenAI wants foregrounded, not the full filings.

This matters for two reasons. First, amicus and supporting briefs are advocacy. People file them because they hold a position, often a position aligned with a party. A brief from academics arguing fair use supports research is a real data point about one community's view. It is not a measure of consensus across all creators, many of whom are actively suing AI companies on exactly the opposite theory.

Second, the creator community is split, and the post papers over that. There are working artists and writers who see AI training as theft of their life's work, and there are creators who use these tools daily and want the doctrine to stay broad. Both are real. A page that only shows the second group is telling you something true about those people and nothing about the balance.

## Why does this come out as a blog post instead of a filing?

Because the audience isn't the court. The court already has the actual briefs. The audience is everyone else: journalists, policymakers, developers, and the general public forming an opinion while the litigation grinds on. A blog post that says "look how many good people agree with us" is cheaper and travels further than a 40-page legal argument.

That's not a criticism of OpenAI for doing it. Every party in high-stakes litigation runs a public narrative in parallel with the legal one. It's a reason for readers to recalibrate. The post is shaping the environment the eventual ruling lands in. When you see coverage that treats this as "experts confirm AI training is fair use," that's the narrative working exactly as intended.

The honest state of play, as of this writing, is that fair use for model training is unresolved in U.S. courts. There are multiple active cases. Some rulings have gone partly one way, some another, and none of it is final. Anyone telling you the question is settled, in either direction, is selling something. OpenAI's post leans optimistic because optimism serves OpenAI. That's allowed. It's just not the same as a verdict.

## What should a builder take from this?

If you ship anything on top of large models, the copyright question is a live risk sitting under your product, and you don't get to resolve it by reading a sympathetic blog post. Here's the practical read.

Watch the actual cases, not the press releases. The language that matters will come from judges, in orders and opinions, not from curated brief excerpts on any lab's website. Follow the dockets or follow legal reporters who do, and treat first-party blog framing from any party, OpenAI included, as position rather than fact.

Think about your own exposure separately. Whether OpenAI wins its fair use argument says little about your liability for how you fine-tune, what data you feed in, or what your model outputs reproduce. If you train or fine-tune on scraped or licensed data, your situation has its own four-factor analysis. "OpenAI said fair use benefits everyone" is not a defense you can file.

And notice the pattern, because you'll see it again. A lab under legal pressure publishes a page of friendly voices, the framing leaks into coverage as consensus, and the harder question quietly recedes. The catch most readers miss is that the post never actually argues the model training is legal. It argues that fair use is good and that good people support it, and lets you connect the dots yourself. Make the lab prove the specific claim, not the general mood.

Practitioner's take: bookmark OpenAI's "Fair use benefits everyone" post as a clean example of litigation-adjacent messaging, then go read what the courts have actually held before you build any plan around it. For your own product, assume the copyright question is unresolved, keep records of your data sources and licensing, and don't let a blog post stand in for legal advice you should get from someone who files briefs instead of excerpting them.
