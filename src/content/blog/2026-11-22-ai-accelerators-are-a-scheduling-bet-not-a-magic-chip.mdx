TL;DR: Choose AI hardware by where your workload’s scheduling decision belongs: branching code wants CPUs, wide regular tensor work wants GPUs, and stable model graphs may fit dataflow accelerators.

## What does “runs next” really mean?

IBM Technology’s “AI Accelerators vs. CPUs: Why They Think Differently” has a clean framing I wish showed up in more AI infrastructure conversations: the key question is not “which chip is faster?” It is “who decides what runs next?”

A CPU answers that question through an instruction stream, a small number of powerful cores, branch prediction, caching, out-of-order execution, and all the machinery needed to make messy software behave correctly. Operating systems, Python glue, databases, web servers, pointer-heavy code, small functions, unpredictable branches. This is the CPU’s home turf.

A GPU answers the same question differently. It still runs instructions, but the win comes from applying the same operation across many data elements at once. Instead of asking which single operation is ready in a small instruction window, a GPU scheduler looks for a ready warp or wave, often groups of 32 threads, and swaps work in when other work is waiting on memory.

That is why the CPU vs. GPU debate gets silly when people talk in generalities. If the workload is one add and one multiply on one data element, the GPU does not magically matter. If the workload is huge regular tensor math, the CPU’s control machinery becomes expensive overhead.

## Where do dataflow accelerators fit?

IBM Technology describes dataflow-style AI accelerators as programmable, but aimed at a smaller and useful subset of compute patterns, especially AI models. The scheduling decision moves closer to the compute graph itself. Instead of the chip constantly asking which instruction or warp is ready, the graph structure says when operations can fire because their inputs are ready.

That sounds abstract, but it matters for model serving and training because neural networks are mostly graphs of tensor operations. Feed data through layers. Multiply, add, normalize, activate, repeat. There are branches and dynamic pieces in modern systems, but a lot of the heavy math is regular.

This is the real tradeoff. CPUs are flexible and latency-oriented. GPUs are throughput-oriented and general enough to dominate much of AI. Dataflow accelerators can be very efficient when the model’s shape, memory movement, and operation set fit the chip’s assumptions.

The catch is in that word “fit.” A smaller supported compute pattern can be a feature if you run the right workload all day. It can also become a tax if your model changes, your operators are unsupported, your compiler path is immature, or your serving stack spends more time in preprocessing, retrieval, routing, and Python overhead than in dense math.

## What should builders test before betting on hardware?

I would not start with vendor charts. I would start with a profile.

For inference, measure where latency actually goes. Token generation may be GPU-bound, but your product path may include auth, retrieval, reranking, tool calls, JSON parsing, safety filters, network hops, and database writes. A faster accelerator does not fix a slow orchestration layer.

For training or fine-tuning, look at utilization, memory bandwidth, batch size, sequence length, and whether the model uses operations the hardware handles well. The best chip on paper can lose if the software stack forces awkward rewrites or leaves the compute units idle.

For agents, the answer may be even less glamorous. Many agent workloads are not one giant matrix multiply. They are a loop: call a model, inspect state, search, call a tool, wait on an API, repair an error, call a model again. The CPU and the network are still in the room.

My practical rule: match the chip to the shape of the bottleneck, not the logo on the accelerator. If you are serving a stable, high-volume model with predictable tensor paths, test GPUs against any dataflow option that supports your stack. If you are building a product with lots of branching, tools, retrieval, and changing models, keep the system flexible until profiling proves the accelerator is the constraint. The catch most teams miss is that “AI workload” is not one workload. It is usually five workloads stitched together, and only some of them want exotic silicon.
