Lexicography sounds niche until you look at the work. Then it starts to look like half the AI product questions builders are wrestling with.

A dictionary entry is not just a chunk of generated prose. It is a decision about meaning, usage, register, culture, examples, frequency, and change over time. It carries authority. It also gets used by students, writers, translators, search systems, language learners, courts, and other software.

That makes AI-assisted lexicography a good stress test for “human in the loop,” a phrase that often means very little in product specs.

A new arXiv paper on human-centered AI in lexicography frames the problem around four dimensions: the augmented lexicographer, the sociotechnical context of adoption, bias, and tool design. The core claim is simple: AI should augment lexicographers, not replace them, even when automation is high. The harder claim is that “meaningful human control” has to survive contact with deadlines, budgets, interfaces, and management incentives.

## The model can draft. It cannot own the judgment.

Generative AI is obviously useful here. It can suggest definitions. It can cluster corpus examples. It can surface emerging senses. It can propose usage notes, translations, citations, etymological hints, and candidate example sentences. For a lexicographer staring at thousands of corpus lines, that is not trivial.

But the work is not reducible to drafting.

A lexicographer decides whether a use is widespread enough to include. Whether a label like “offensive,” “informal,” “archaic,” or “regional” fits. Whether an example sentence reinforces a stereotype. Whether a definition is circular. Whether a borrowed word is represented with respect for the community that uses it. Whether a minority language gets flattened into the patterns of a larger one.

That is where the arXiv framework is right to focus on professional agency. If the AI proposes and the human merely rubber-stamps, the workflow has already shifted power. The interface may still show an “approve” button, but the real control has moved upstream to the model, the training data, the defaults, and the production quota.

## Bias is not only in the model

The paper also makes a useful move by treating bias as part of a sociotechnical system, not just a bad-output problem. That matters.

In dictionary work, bias can come from corpus selection. Which publications count as evidence? Which dialects are overrepresented? Which speakers are missing? Which “standard” gets treated as neutral? AI can amplify those choices because it is good at making dominant patterns feel natural.

Model behavior is only one layer. Procurement is another. So is staffing. So is review policy. So is whether the tool makes uncertainty visible or hides it behind polished prose. So is whether lexicographers can challenge, edit, reject, and trace suggestions without fighting the product.

This is the part many AI workflow pitches skip. They show the happy path: upload corpus, generate entry, ship faster. The real question is what happens when the model is confidently bland, culturally wrong, or statistically right in a way that harms the dictionary’s mission.

The same applies outside lexicography. Legal drafting, clinical documentation, curriculum design, policy analysis, customer support knowledge bases: all of these have “definition-like” moments where language hardens into institutional truth. Speed helps. Bad defaults scale.

A builder should read this as a product design warning. If you are making AI tools for expert language work, do not start with “replace the document.” Start with the decision map. What can the model draft, rank, cluster, or retrieve? What must the expert decide? Where should uncertainty, provenance, and disagreement appear? Then instrument the workflow so rejection is cheap and review is real. The catch most teams miss: human control is not a checkbox. It is a cost you either design for up front or quietly remove in the name of productivity.
