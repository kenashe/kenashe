TL;DR: AI can help blockchain investigators move faster across messy transaction graphs, but attribution still depends on evidence chains, human judgment, and what the tracing firm is willing to show.

## What did Chainalysis reportedly use AI to do?

The named source here is Decrypt’s report, “Chainalysis Used AI to Trace the $387M Bitget Hack Back to North Korea.” Decrypt reported that Chainalysis said it used in-house AI to trace funds from a Sept. 24 Bitget breach across four blockchains, attributing the activity to North Korea and saying the incident pushed North Korea’s 2026 crypto haul past $1 billion.

That is a big claim. It is also exactly the kind of claim where wording matters.

No first-party Chainalysis technical writeup was included here, so I would not treat the model architecture, confidence thresholds, attribution process, or exact investigative steps as confirmed. What we have is trade reporting on what Chainalysis said it did. That is still useful, but it is not the same as a reproducible forensic record.

The interesting part is not “AI found the hackers.” That framing is too cute. The more realistic version is that AI helped investigators sort through a large, moving graph while attackers were still trying to move, swap, bridge, and obscure the stolen assets.

That is a good AI use case. Not because models are mystical. Because the work is high-volume, time-sensitive, and pattern-heavy.

## Where does AI actually fit in crypto tracing?

Blockchain tracing has always been part database work, part graph analysis, part adversarial investigation. The hard part is not seeing one transaction. The hard part is following thousands of related movements across chains, wallets, exchanges, bridges, mixers, and timing patterns without drowning in noise.

AI can help in a few practical ways. It can cluster wallets that behave similarly. It can flag transaction paths that resemble prior laundering playbooks. It can prioritize which branches of a graph look most urgent. It can summarize movement across chains for investigators who need to make decisions quickly. It can also help compare new activity against older patterns linked to known threat groups.

![stolen digital assets splitting into many branching paths across separate networks, with a small investigator node follo](../../assets/blog/2026-10-03-chainalysiss-ai-tracing-claim-is-a-workflow-story-not-magic-attribution/inline-1.webp)

But attribution is a higher bar than tracing. Saying “these funds moved through this path” is different from saying “this state-backed group did it.” North Korea-linked crypto theft has a long public record, but each new attribution should still rest on multiple signals: wallet reuse, infrastructure, malware links, laundering style, exchange touchpoints, timing, and intelligence outside the chain.

AI may speed up the hunt. It does not remove the need for receipts.

## Why this matters beyond one hack

If Decrypt’s reporting is accurate, the operational point is speed. In crypto theft, every hour matters. Funds can be split, swapped, bridged, wrapped, and pushed through liquidity venues before victims, exchanges, and law enforcement can coordinate. A tool that narrows the search space fast can change what gets frozen, flagged, or recovered.

That is also where the hype risk lives. “AI tracing” sounds cleaner than the underlying work. Models can make bad clusters. They can overfit to old laundering patterns. They can miss novel behavior. They can produce confident summaries that hide weak assumptions. In investigations, those errors are not cosmetic. They can affect accusations, compliance actions, and public narratives.

For builders, the lesson is not limited to crypto. The same pattern applies to fraud ops, security alerts, abuse detection, and compliance review: use AI to compress time-to-triage, not to replace the evidentiary chain. Start with one workflow where analysts already inspect noisy graphs or event streams. Add model-assisted clustering, path ranking, and case summaries. Keep the raw trail clickable. The catch most teams miss: the model output is only valuable if every recommendation can be checked fast by a human who knows what would make it wrong.
