TL;DR: For multi-turn agents, rewarding only the final answer can train models to take unsafe shortcuts, so reward design and evaluation need to score the information-gathering process too.

## What goes wrong when the reward only checks the final answer?

The primary source here is the arXiv paper **“Safe Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Turn Clinical Agents: When Dense Rewards Beat Outcome Rewards”**. It studies reinforcement learning in clinical agents that have to do more than answer a static question. They must decide what to ask, when to stop asking, and what action to take.

That matters. A lot of agent work still treats success as a final-state problem. Did the model get the answer right? Did the ticket close? Did the workflow complete? Did the simulated patient get the right action?

The paper shows the trap: outcome-only group relative policy optimization can improve aggregate reward while teaching bad procedure. The agents learned patterns like premature commitment, one-question collapse, and missed behavior on rare high-risk cases. In plain English, the agent can look better on the scoreboard while becoming worse at the kind of caution that makes the system safe.

Clinical tasks make this easy to see because the cost of a bad shortcut is obvious. But the pattern is not medical-only. Any multi-turn agent that gathers evidence before acting has the same failure mode. Customer support escalation. Security triage. Legal intake. Loan review. Incident response. If the reward only scores the final outcome, the model may learn the cheapest path to a pass, not the process a competent operator would trust.

## Why did dense rewards help?

The paper introduces three controlled environments: medication refill triage, acute diagnostic work-up, and dosing titration. Each has hidden clinical states, query costs, exact adaptive oracles, and deterministic outcome verifiers. That setup is useful because it lets the researchers separate “got the final answer” from “gathered the right evidence adaptively.”

The key result is in diagnostics. Assigning dense credit to questions that reduce uncertainty improved held-out value and moderate-case support across three training seeds compared with outcome-only reinforcement learning. It also avoided the high-risk seed collapse seen under outcome-only training.

This is not a blanket win for more process reward. The paper reports that the right amount of process credit is task-dependent. Separating exploration from exploitation improved high-risk support at its best checkpoint, but also caused excessive information gathering on low-acuity cases. That is the real tradeoff. You can make an agent more careful, then discover it now asks too many questions when the case is simple.

That sounds annoying. It is also honest. Safety in multi-turn systems is usually not a single scalar you maximize. It is a budgeted behavior: ask enough, stop when you should, and do not average away the rare cases.

## What should agent builders take from this?

The biggest warning is that reinforcement learning did not reliably repair rare behavior gaps inherited from biased supervised data. After a supervised warmup that already gathered information carefully, reinforcement learning raised aggregate reward by trimming questions, while degrading high-risk performance.

That should make builders suspicious of “RL will fix it” as a default story. If the supervised policy underrepresents rare but important cases, outcome-based RL may not rediscover them. It may instead compress the procedure around the common cases that pay off most often.

So the evaluation has to inspect the path. Not just final accuracy. Not just average reward. The paper’s language is precise: evaluation must distinguish adaptive information gathering from reward-exploiting shortcuts.

For a practical agent, I would log and score at least three things separately: what the agent asked, when it stopped, and whether its final action was justified by the evidence it collected. Then I would slice those results by risk tier, not only aggregate pass rate. The catch most readers miss: dense rewards are not magic safety dust. They are a design surface. If you reward “ask more” too hard, you get sluggish agents. If you reward final outcomes too hard, you get lucky shortcuts. The work is tuning the procedure you actually want, then testing the rare cases where averages lie.
