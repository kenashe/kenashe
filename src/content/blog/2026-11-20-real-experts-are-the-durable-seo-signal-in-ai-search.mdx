TL;DR: If AI search compresses the web into answers, the sites that keep earning visibility will be the ones with real people, real expertise, and evidence that cannot be faked at scale.

## What changes when AI search becomes the interface?

Lily Ray’s Search Engine Journal piece, “Why Building Trust Through Real People Stands The Test Of Time In SEO & AI Search,” makes a point that should feel obvious but still gets ignored: scaled AI content, fake authors, and self-promotional listicles can work for a while. Then the floor moves.

That has always been true in search. Tactics get copied, inflated, detected, discounted, and eventually become a liability. The AI-search version is sharper because the user may never see ten blue links. They see a synthesized answer, a few citations, maybe a brand mention, maybe nothing. If your page is interchangeable filler, you are not just competing with other publishers. You are competing with the answer itself.

This does not mean “AI content is bad.” That is too lazy. AI can help with outlines, research organization, rewriting rough notes, content ops, internal linking, and QA. The weak move is using it to manufacture authority. Fake experts. Fake review experience. Fake “best” lists written by nobody who touched the product. Thin pages with confident language and no accountable person behind them.

AI search makes that more fragile, not less. If an answer engine has to decide which claims to compress, it has every reason to prefer content tied to identifiable people, original experience, known entities, and corroborated signals outside the page.

## What does “real people” mean operationally?

“Real people” is not a headshot slapped onto a page. It is a system.

A credible expert page should connect a person to a topic, a track record, and the content they are responsible for. A credible article should show why that person can speak on the subject. A credible review should make clear what was tested, what was not tested, and where judgment enters the picture.

The practical bar is higher for YMYL topics, but the pattern applies everywhere. If you publish about SaaS, who has implemented the software? If you publish about marketing, who has run the campaigns? If you publish about domains, who has negotiated names, built sites, or watched rankings move after a migration? If the answer is “our content team researched it,” say that. Just do not dress it up as firsthand expertise.

The catch: this is slower than spinning up 500 pages from a keyword export. That is why it lasts longer. Real authority has a cost structure that spam does not like.

## Where should builders spend effort now?

Start with the pages that matter most. Not the whole site. Pick the pages that drive revenue, trust, or discovery. Then ask: would a skeptical reader know who made this, why they should trust it, and what evidence supports it?

Add named authors only when they are real. Strengthen bios with specific experience, not vague credentials. Link expert profiles to related work. Put original photos, screenshots, examples, tests, constraints, and dates where they help the reader verify the claim. Remove fake certainty. If something is an opinion, label it as one. If something is based on testing, show the test.

This also changes how teams should use AI. The model can draft from raw expert notes. It can turn a call transcript into a first pass. It can find missing sections or unclear claims. But the value comes from the human inputs: field notes, customer conversations, product use, failed experiments, decisions made under constraints.

Practitioner’s Take: take one important article this week and run a trust audit on it. Who is the accountable person? What firsthand evidence is visible? Which claims sound generic enough that any competitor could publish them? Use AI to speed the cleanup, but do not let it invent authority. The missed catch is that “helpful content” is not a writing style. It is proof of contact with the real world.
