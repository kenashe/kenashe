TL;DR: As of today, the EU AI Act's transparency rules require that AI-generated images, audio, video, and certain text be marked as such, but the scope is narrower and messier than the "mark everything" panic suggests.

The loudest take I saw this morning came from r/LocalLLaMA, where a post titled "EU AI Act takes effect tomorrow, August 2, 2026. 🤡" summed up the mood: "Basically you now have to mark all AI generated images, audio, video and text as AI generated." The clown emoji did the editorializing. That reaction is understandable and also mostly wrong on the details. So let me separate what the law says from what a frustrated forum thread says it says.

I want to be honest about sourcing here. The primary material I have is that Reddit post, not the consolidated text of the Regulation. So I am going to be careful: where I describe the Act's actual structure, I am drawing on what the Regulation has said publicly since it passed, and I will flag where the community's summary and the legal text diverge. If you are making compliance decisions, read the actual Article text, not this post and definitely not a meme thread.

## What actually took effect on August 2?

The EU AI Act did not switch on all at once. It phases in. The prohibited-practices rules and AI literacy obligations landed earlier, in February 2025. The general-purpose AI model obligations came in August 2025. The date people are reacting to now, August 2, 2026, is another milestone in that staged rollout, and the transparency obligations for AI-generated content sit in this general timeline.

The relevant piece is Article 50, the transparency rules. In plain terms, Article 50 says two things that matter for builders. First, providers of AI systems that generate synthetic audio, image, video, or text have to mark their outputs in a machine-readable way so they can be detected as artificially generated. Second, deployers who use AI to produce deepfakes, or text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, have to disclose that the content is artificial.

Notice what that is not. It is not "label every sentence ChatGPT helped you write." The obligations are split between providers (the people building the model or system) and deployers (the people using it), and they carve out real exceptions.

## Do you really have to label everything?

No, and this is where the Reddit summary overshoots.

The text-labeling requirement is narrow. It applies to text "published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest." That is news, essentially. A marketing email, your app's autocomplete, code your copilot wrote, a customer-service reply: those are not "informing the public on matters of public interest" in the sense the Article targets. And even for the news case, there is an exception where the AI content has undergone human review or editorial control and a person holds editorial responsibility.

There are also exemptions where the AI performs an "assistive function for standard editing" or does not substantially alter the input. Grammar cleanup, formatting, small edits: not the target.

For images and video, the deepfake disclosure obligation kicks in when content "appreciably resembles" real people, objects, places, or events and would falsely appear authentic. There are carve-outs for artistic, creative, and satirical work, where the disclosure just has to not spoil the experience.

So the accurate version of the Reddit claim is: providers must embed machine-readable markers in synthetic outputs, and deployers must disclose deepfakes and AI-written public-interest news. That is a much smaller footprint than "mark everything, lol."

## What are the real hard parts?

The genuine difficulty is not the labeling philosophy. It is the machine-readable marking requirement on the provider side.

The Act wants markers that are "effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as technically feasible." Read the hedge in that sentence. Everyone in the room knows watermarking is not solved. C2PA content credentials can be stripped. Invisible image watermarks degrade under compression, cropping, and screenshotting. Text watermarking is even shakier and can be washed out by light paraphrasing. The law is asking for something the state of the art cannot fully deliver, and it knows it, which is why the "technically feasible" escape hatch exists.

![a watermark pattern slowly dissolving as an image passes through compression and cropping stages](../../assets/blog/2026-08-02-the-eu-ai-acts-transparency-rules-are-live-what-actually-changed-on-august-2/inline-2.png)

The second hard part is that the detailed how is still being written. The AI Office is developing codes of practice and guidelines for the marking and detection methods. Until those settle, "compliant" is a moving target. That uncertainty is exactly why the tone online is anxious. Nobody likes shipping against a spec that is half-drafted.

The third: enforcement timing versus obligation timing. An obligation being "in effect" is not the same as a regulator kicking down your door on day one. The penalty machinery and national competent authorities ramp up on their own schedule. The rational reading is that early enforcement targets clear bad actors and large providers, not a solo dev using a local model for a Discord bot. That is not legal advice, it is pattern-matching on how EU regulation historically rolls out.

## Does this touch local and open-source models?

This is the r/LocalLLaMA question underneath the clown emoji, and it is a fair one.

If you run a model locally to generate images or text for your own use, you are largely the deployer, and the deployer obligations only bite in the specific cases above: deepfakes of real people, public-interest news. Private tinkering is not the target.

The friction lands on providers. If you release an open model, are you on the hook for embedding output markers? The Act's language centers on providers "placing on the market or putting into service" generative systems. Open-weights releases have historically gotten some breathing room in the Act relative to closed commercial systems, but "generate synthetic content" obligations are one area where the open-source carve-outs are thinner than the community would like. This is a real tension, not a settled question, and it is worth watching how the guidelines treat a raw weights drop versus a hosted generation API.

The honest summary: local users, mostly fine. Open model publishers, genuinely uncertain, and that uncertainty is legitimate rather than a joke.

Here is how I would actually respond as a builder shipping into the EU. If you run a hosted service that generates images, audio, or video, turn on C2PA content credentials now, because it is the closest thing to a recognized standard and shows good-faith effort under the "technically feasible" clause. If you publish AI-written news or public-facing informational content, add a visible disclosure and keep a record of human editorial review, which is your exemption. If you are a solo dev or local-model hobbyist, do not panic-label your grocery lists; the text rule is about public-interest news, not everything you type. And the catch most readers miss: the standards for machine-readable marking are still being drafted by the AI Office, so the smart move is documenting your reasonable compliance effort today, not chasing a final spec that does not exist yet. Watermarking is not solved, the law knows it, and defensible good faith beats performative labeling.
